When I think about making money, I think about Ronnie Coleman and Warren
Buffett.

> Everybody wants to be a bodybuilder, but don't nobody want to lift no
> heavy-ass weights.

–Ronnie Coleman

What Ronnie is articulating here is the fact that people simply don't want to
do the hard work to get jacked. Similarly, people don't want to do the hard
work (patience, austerity, diligence) to get rich.

> The first rule of investment is don't lose money.

–Warren Buffett

Buffett's axiom can be applied to not losing money in general, not just in the
markets. Truthfully, I'm pretty bad at not *losing money*, but not as bad as
[37% of Americans][SHED], who can't cover a $400 emergency with cash. In the
end, you don't need some cracked hypothesis or market arbitrage to stack cash;
you probably just need to, you know, stop spending so much.

In the following post, I'll break down how I got rich (slowly).

## Dollar-Cost Averaging (DCA)

> *Every month, take some percentage of your income and invest it.*

When I was around 18 or 19, I got obsessed with money. As such, I started to
read books on money. One of the first books I read (or started to read) was
Benjamin Graham's *The Intelligent Investor*. However, you only need to read
the preface by Buffett, which basically says investing well takes a sound
framework and the discipline to keep your emotions out of it. For me, that
framework boils down to index funds, ETFs, bitcoin, and gold. I take
a percentage of my income and simply spread it across these asset classes every
month. The saying *time in the market beats timing the market* is very much at
play here.

## No Credit Card Debt. Period.

> *Pay off all credit card debt.*

I was hunting with this guy once. He had a lot of expensive things: guns,
trucks, gadgets. He also had a ton of student loans. Noticing this, I asked
him: *"Aren't you concerned with all the debt you have?"* His reply: *"Debt
isn't a problem. You just have to manage it."*

This stuck with me due to how profoundly stupid it was. This guy was not
a bank. He was not using leverage to generate value. He was a consumerist dolt.

Regrettably, this has been a problem for me as well (John 8:7). I've accrued
high-interest debt and probably paid many thousands in interest because I lack
the self-control to save for things. In reality, this is the **biggest**
improvement you can make to your financial posture, since credit cards carry
a heavy APR and can only yield negative returns.

You probably shouldn't even think about investing until you've paid off your
credit cards.

## Save 20%

> *Keep monthly expenses at or below 80% of regular take-home pay.*

This is a pretty easy rule: income * 0.2 should be your savings. That means
your expenses should not exceed income * 0.8. This is a practical application
of the first law of making money: *spend less than you earn*.[^1] This will
beat any budget, since you only need to care about two numbers: money in, money
out. Setting up a Rube Goldberg machine of budgets on various different
purchases is a waste of time. Just save 20%.

## Contribute to a Roth IRA

> *Put after-tax money in a Roth IRA every year you're eligible.*

A Roth IRA is an individual investment vehicle that allows individuals to
contribute to their retirement with after-tax money. Upon reaching the
designated age, the money can be taken out tax-free. The only caveat is that
there are contribution limits and income restrictions.

In a [previous article][Fixing Excess IRA Contributions], I detailed how
I overcontributed to seven IRAs across four institutions. So, don't do that.

Open a Vanguard account and start contributing.

## Max Out Your HSA and 401(k)

> *Contribute at least enough to get the full employer match.*

One of the benefits of being a wage slave is the employer match for my health
savings account (HSA) and 401(k). This is literally free money, so figure out
your match and at least contribute to max out the benefit.

Also, an HSA is triple tax-advantaged (going in, growing, and coming out), so
it is an excellent investment vehicle.

It should be noted that both an HSA and a 401(k) have contribution limits. The
HSA limit includes employer contributions, while the 401(k) employee limit
covers only your own.

## Let Your Money Make Money

> *Keep your cash in a high-yield savings account.*

When I was poor and heard people talking about interest rates, I'd quickly do
the math and realize a 3% APY on my savings account was, like, $50. Now that
I'm *rich*, I realize 3% on liquid cash is actually kinda a lot of money. It's
super easy to put your liquid money in a high-yield savings account.

## Why Slowly

Getting rich doesn't require a diamond-hands scheme, *monitoring the
situation*, or some advanced arbitrage. The above is the straight dope on the
financial essentials to get to multi-millionaire status over time (i.e., how
poor, *asset-light* people make it). It *will* work for you.
